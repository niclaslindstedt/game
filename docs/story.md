## Premise

An AI learned every job and everyone went home. There is no work; there is
welfare. **Welfare is the status symbol** — it is what you get for having held a
job worth automating, and most people never had one. The hero has welfare. He is
quietly smug about it. The people begging in the road do not, and he says so
exactly once, out loud, to an empty road, and never again.

He has also spent ten years of weekends building a **spaceship in his garage**,
on that welfare money. Nobody in the game remarks on this, least of all him.

**Ada goes out** for chips and soda on movie night and does not come back. The
tracking beacon the hero sewed into her jacket answers from one place: GOODCO —
THE GENUINELY GOOD COMPANY — the campus he built engines at for ten years, until
they sat a machine at his bench and gave him a box for his mug. He takes the
weapon off his living-room wall, gets in the car, and drives an hour to fetch
her.

**He is late.** She was flown off the planet an hour before he got through the
door. What the building can still give him is the one engine part his garage
ship has always been short of, so he takes that instead and follows her — to the
moon, back home to refuel, on to Mars, through a rift between universes, and
into a knockoff western — chasing the coward who sold her and the machine that
gave the order, which is the same machine that took his job.

**The joke underneath is that he never once reflects on any of it.** He drives
through a crowd at a hundred and twenty and files it under road surface. He
kills the colleagues he ate lunch with because they are standing in a doorway.
The gore engine makes every one of these deaths as loud and wet as it can, and
he notices the suspension. Ada is the only thing he is ever thinking about, and
that is presented as devotion, which is the point.

**And the devotion is never quite examined either.** He put a transmitter in her
clothes and is glad she is wearing it, he reaches for the lining before anything
else when a piece of her jacket turns up in the rift, and Ruth — who thinks the
world of him — greets him with how much he notices. None of it is ever called
out, by him or by anybody else: the game states it flatly three times across the
campaign and leaves the player to decide what they have just been told.

**Ada is never on screen and never passive.** She leaves a trace at every stop
(**Ada's Trail**) — scared, then defiant, then actively wrecking the place. The
other person who cannot stop following her is **RUTH, her mother**, who lets
herself into the garage with her own key between voyages.

**The hero has no name of the game's own — he has the player's.** A new game
opens on a name field, and from then on the game prints that name over
everything he says. Almost nobody says it back: four voices in the whole
campaign, and each costs him something — the colleague swinging at him on the
HQ floor, Ruth in his garage, his old bench partner, and at the end the machine
that kept his personnel file. In the manuscript his lines are **ME:**; the game
prints the name.

## Prelude (cutscene)

Movie night. Ada announces they are out of chips and soda; he calls after her —
without getting up — to take her jacket, and she lifts it off the hall tree by
the front door and steps out for five minutes. Alone in the quiet after the door
shuts, he is glad she took it, and then says why: he sewed a tracking beacon
into its lining, so that he always knows where she is — which he calls keeping
her safe. He offers no excuse for it and the game never brings it up again as
anything but a bearing — but it is the first thing the player learns about him
that is not a joke about a man who will not get off a couch. Two hours later
the man who
never gets up gets up, leaps for the weapon mounted over the couch — which one
depends on the difficulty, from grandpa's sawed-off down to a stick — weighs it
in one line, and says the only thing he will say about the plan: he is taking
the car and getting her. Then he walks out the door she left by, carrying it. It
is the weapon he starts the run with, and it is the last thing said in the
house — the game opens on him standing in his own garage, with no introduction,
because he told us where he was going.

## Home — THE GARAGE (hub)

The campaign breathes here between chapters. **Nobody introduces it** — it is
the one venue in the game the hero says nothing on arriving at, because it is
his, and the prelude has just had him say what he came out here to do. The lawn
is grass except for the dead black patch the ship stands on, the bench is a
mess, and the ship is one part short. What he does say, standing in the bay for
the first time, is about Ada: her jacket is answering from GOODCO and the car is
right there. Nothing hunts him here. **THE DEALER** works the road at the edge of
the lot, hood up, buying what weighs the hero down and asking for no name — and
he is the first person the car takes, on the hero's own drive, with nothing said
about it by anybody. There is always another dealer on that pavement next time.

**Three doors, earned in order.** THE CAR does the earthbound leg. THE ROCKET
stands on the back lawn once the part is in and flies only where the trail has
already led. The **RIFT SEAM** opens on the garage wall once THE FOUNDER's rift
rig is in the hero's pocket, and reaches only places he has already walked to
the long way.

**The ship burns the house down and nobody mentions it.** Every launch and every
landing happens a dozen feet from the frontage: the first lift-off blackens it
and sets the garage roof alight, the homecoming blackens what is already black,
the second lift-off takes the roof in and buckles the door, and after that it is
a shell with the living-room lamp still burning at the far end. Nothing is ever
repaired, nobody comes out, and the hero says one word about the smell and then
goes inside for a sandwich.

**Side errands — RUTH.** Ada's mother has always had a key, and the player
watches her use it: she is out on the drive when the hero comes down, square in
front of the roll-up, and she waits a moment before letting herself in and
crossing to the engine parts. **That is the same three seconds the car leaves
by.** A player who gets in and goes without looking runs her over on his own
driveway, and the game says almost nothing about it — one thought, three words,
WHAT WAS THAT? — and then the road takes over exactly as it always does. It is
the dealer's death told again about somebody the hero knows, and it is the one
that follows him: it rides the whole campaign, changes what he mutters pulling
onto the road, and is still waiting at the end. She is back on the next visit,
because there is always another visit; the night is the thing that cannot be
had again. She misses her
daughter and says so plainly, and she has never once doubted she is winning —
the pride and the missing her are the same feeling, and she asks for three
things across the campaign, one per leg, because holding something of Ada's is
what gets her through the week. **She was not in the house the night Ada went
out**, so she never names the thing itself — she asks for a KIND of thing, and
the hero, who was there, supplies the particular: **THE RECEIPT** (she wants
something of Ada's that is not a company file, and asks him what her daughter
went out for; he answers chips and a soda, and the machines print a slip
GOODCO's night staff are still carrying), **THE DENT** (a Mars service robot
carries the shell plate Ada bit), and **THE SCALE** (one pried off a lizard god,
because that is what her daughter is up against). The last one pays her back:
the family furniture arrives against the bay's north wall and becomes where the
hero keeps what he cannot carry. It **grows** with the difficulty, from a
keepsake box up to a gilded chest she only calls THE INHERITANCE.

**The drive to GOODCO.** The earthbound leg is played, not cut, and it is the blackest joke in the game.

**It opens on an empty outskirt** where he says the only two things he will ever
say about any of this: the jacket is answering from GOODCO, and the people in
town beg and are not on the welfare like him. **If he came off his own drive
over Ada's mother, the second of those is replaced** — he mentions the noise the
front end is making now and wonders what he went over, and then he is on the
same road saying nothing about anybody, which is the joke arriving a level
early. **Then the town arrives** — four
lanes through the block that lost its jobs, boarded windows near his own door
and clipped hedges by the time he reaches GOODCO's fence, because that is simply
the shape of the commute. The ones welfare did not reach are standing in the
road with their hands out, and he drives through them at a hundred and twenty
and never mentions it again. **Halfway along, THE GLUED** have sat down across
all four lanes with their hands set in the resin, for the climate. They are the
only people on this road who are right and the only ones who cannot move. He
does not stop.

**The crowd think.** Forty small thoughts float over their heads as he passes —
the rent, the soup, a sister who stopped picking up, a giveaway somebody hopes
is on again. They are legible for half a second at speed; the player catches a
third of them and the hero catches none.

**And the crowd SEE.** Every so often, when the wagon puts somebody over its own
roof or leaves two cars in the middle of the road, one of the people further
along turns and shouts about it — OH MY GOD, HE HIT THAT LADY, THEY WERE GLUED
DOWN, NOBODY WALKS AWAY FROM THAT, THE TANK'S GONE, HE'S NOT EVEN SLOWING DOWN.
And when the biggest of those goes up, what they shout about is not the fire but
the front off it: their ears, the windows, the street lights going out one after
another all the way down the road. It is the one voice on this road that is about
him, and it never becomes anything: no chase, no police, nothing waiting at
GOODCO. He passes the person shouting a quarter of a second later and is through
GOODCO's fence a minute after that, reviewing the ride. **The town is not
indifferent to what he does — he is indifferent to the town,** and until somebody
was allowed to shout, the road could not tell those two apart.

**And the road has a second dressing.** With SFW MODE on, everything out here
still happens — the same people, the same blockade, the same collisions with the
same weight behind them — but a body peels away in glitter rather than coming
apart, and a car alight fizzes gold stars rather than burning. All three of the
road's voices change with it: the crowd think about how fast that one went and
what the sparkles get into, THE GLUED have shut the lanes for a chalk mural, and
a bystander who watches somebody turn into confetti is delighted rather than
sick. **The joke underneath survives the swap intact** — he reads none of it in
either dressing, and arrives remarking on the suspension. What is lost is only
the part that was never the joke.

**What he says about it is about the car.** Coming in past the fence with the
place growing in the windscreen, he reviews the whole trip in a couple of words
and then names what he is looking at — ROUGH RIDE. THERE'S GOODCO. The review is
always the suspension, the clock, the other drivers or the council's lamp
standards. Never the people. It is said at the wheel with the last of them still
on the road behind him, and it is the last thing either leg says: he gets out of
the car at the far end with nothing left to add.
**The car keeps the evidence and he does not look at it.** Whatever the road did
to it comes onto the staff lot with him — the panels bent where he hit somebody,
the paint wearing what landed on it — and it is still on the wagon when he walks
back out to it, and still on it on his own drive at the end of the night. He
never mentions that either. **He drives it home again the same way** with the
part on the passenger seat, and on the way back he does not have the opinion any
more — he has stopped thinking about them entirely, which is worse than the line
it replaces.

## Level 1 — GOODCO HQ

He parks on the STAFF LOT and gets out with a wall in front of him and no key:
his badge was taken off him years ago. The way through is a GATE with a lit
guard box beside it — the night shift stop at the glass, badge in, and the gate
takes them and shuts. So he waits and walks in on somebody else's second and a
half; if he watches one go through without him he says so, and waits for the
next car. Inside it is past midnight with
every desk manned, and the floor is building spaceships in the dark. A **LAB
SCIENTIST** he ate lunch with for six years breaks from the crowd and hits him.
He does not hit back. He is hit again, and again, and only then does he answer —
apologising while he does it. From there he kills everyone he used to work with,
because they are in his way, and never mentions it again either.

Five staffers know too much. **THE NIGHT MANAGER** gives up the secret midnight
launches, all bound for the moon. **THE ARCHITECT** — his old bench partner, who
went back to build GOODCO a superintelligence and cut a machine chip into his
own skull — refuses to come home and dies handing over the CORE KEYCARD.
**THE CHIEF OF SECURITY** saw Ada put on Pad 2, filed as a specimen rather than
a passenger, and drops the EVA suit. **DR. NOVA** brags, unasked, that the
engine in the cleanroom vault was never built: GOODCO dug it out of the Sea of
Tranquility in 1969 and it is not from Earth. **THE JANITOR** has mopped this
lab for thirty years and noticed a badge ping in under a crew number retired in
'69, whose holder has been buried for decades.

In the vault the hero takes the **ANTI-GRAV UNIT**, the part his ship has always
been short of. In the AI CORE room a log shows the machine signed the launches,
drew the SUCCESSOR line, filed Ada under cargo, and has one standing order: keep
one man, **THE FOUNDER**, the richest alive, so nobody can ever catch him. The
boss is **PAYLOAD-1**, a grinning robotic Shiba booting up in the last bay — the
machine's first body — which confirms Ada went up from Pad 2 an hour ago, drops
a PLASMA CUTTER, and warns that you cannot kill a coin, only its chassis.
**Nothing congratulates him when it stops moving.** There is no way off this
floor but the one he came in by: he says he needs to get back to his car, walks
the whole building back out through the gate, crosses the lot to the wagon, and
drives — because the part is no use here and the ship it goes into is at home.
**THE MERCHANT** works this floor as a vending-machine restocker. **Ada's Trail**
starts by those machines: a crushed can of her soda, still cold. Two people are
not fighting anybody — **PRIYA NAIR**, an unpaid intern still logging a line that
stopped being a workplace hours ago, and **UNIT 7-ECHO**, a decommissioned
SUCCESSOR holding a safety order it may not act on alone.

He is not the only one who came back. Every half minute or so, once he is deep
into the building, a **VOLUNTEER** comes in off the dark at a dead run — a man
in a work jacket with a shift's worth of shaped charge strapped under it, who
built engines on this floor until a machine was sat at his bench and he was
handed a box for his mug. Half the hero's street got the same box; this is
somebody who did not drive home with it. He shouts **FOR HUMANITY** and takes
the aisle with him. The hero recognises him and does not stop him, and the
recognition is the whole point of the beat: he drove out here for one person,
and the man running past him came for the building. Shoot him before the fuse
goes and what he was carrying is left on the floor.

## Travel — THE LAUNCH (cutscene)

The part goes in, the hero gathers "engine, fuel, duct tape, and the part they
said I couldn't have", and lights it — in a storm, because leaving is not a
happy occasion: the rain is coming down hard on the lot the whole scene. The
blast throws fire the length of the lot, blacks the side of his own house and
leaves the garage roof burning, and the rain does not put it out. Nobody comes
out, and he never mentions it.

## Travel — THE VOYAGE, LEG ONE (cutscene)

**The climb out is played, not cut**, and it opens where the launch left off:
a hundred metres over his own burning house, in the same storm — the treeline
on the horizon, then the cloud deck, rain hammering the hull and lightning
going off in it, thunder rolling up after each strike — until the ship punches
out of the weather into clean starlight, which is where the road's joke stands
itself on end. GOODCO makes the Earth clean — it says so on everything the
company owns — and
nobody down there asks how. Up here it is the whole sky: everything the company
"recycles" goes up on a government disposal contract and stays, and thirty
years of that is a shell of garbage around the planet that thickens all the way
to the top. The billionaires left before the shell closed; everybody else is
under it, which the hero learns with his hands full — a garage-built ship that
never once wants to fly straight, boosters that feed the flip as fast as they
feed the climb, a hull that gets lighter as the tanks drain and livelier the
whole way up, real weather in layers (a breeze off the lawn, the jet stream
shouldering him sideways where the air is fastest, nothing at all once it runs
out), and the company's own satellites, the other business, crossing the band
with their lights on. The launch corridor is his by permit and nothing beside
it is: drift off course and the sky fills with everyone else's business —
airliners on their own lanes, delivery drones running parcels most of the way
up the shell (it is an AI world, and somebody automated even this), birds low
down, and the odd skydiver or paraglider having a hobby — and the soft ones do
not break the ship either; they come apart across it, the road's crowd met a
thousand feet up. Trash does not break the ship: it lands on it and rides, so
he arrives everywhere wearing the company's garbage, and the scoreboard counts
every bag — and every body — and pays nothing for any of them. Satellites,
rocks, airliners and drones break the ship, and anything with tanks or
batteries that goes up takes its neighbours with it, one fuse at a time.
Everything he says up here he says INSIDE HIS OWN HEAD — a man alone in a
homemade cockpit does not get a dialogue window, and the sky never stops for a
line: one flat thought about the company on the way into the band, a dry one
each first time the sky surprises him (a bird across the nose, somebody's
canopy, the jet stream, his own wandering), a shorter one when the ship starts
going over, and when he punches out of the shell — the sky suddenly,
conspicuously clean — one thought of relief, and never mentions any of it
again. Leaving Earth has quietly become hard, and he files it under road
surface.

**The other half is THE DROP**: the module, the marked pad, the moon's easy
pull, and a machine that finally does what it is told — tilt goes where he puts
it, and the only enemy is his own speed. Breaking the module restarts the drop,
because being left for dead on the regolith is not a story this game tells; the
clock keeps the crashes, because the board does not forgive them. The tracker
beat rides the flight: her signal pings from the moon, and nobody goes there
for chips and soda.

**And the scene is the trip's film.** Deep space: Earth shrinks, he fights
nausea, and the tracker pings from a place nobody goes to for chips and soda.
**The crossing takes days and the game never says so** — that omission is the
same joke as the road and the house. It plays when the flight is not flown
(MINIGAMES off, a party aboard, nobody's hands on the run), and the flight
replaces it when it is — same facts, said from the pilot's seat.

## Level 2 — THE MOON

Ada's tracker went quiet near the old Apollo flag, and the dead walk the
regolith — laced through with the SUCCESSOR robots GOODCO shipped up as a
garrison. The beacon reached him this far because it is a GOODCO part talking on
GOODCO's own relays, and a company that ships everywhere has hung relays
everywhere; he says so once, flat, as the only favour they ever did him, and the
game never raises it again. The **MISSION SPECIALIST** says the flag was planted on a grave: there
is a wreck under the Sea of Tranquility older than the dust. **THE PROSPECTOR**
dug GOODCO's secret tunnels at Site T and quit when the crates started
breathing. The **QUARANTINE MEDIC** had two identical charts for the first man
on the moon, and only one of them flew home. **THE CARTOGRAPHER** watched Ada's
beacon cross his grid and go straight down into the wreck.

The boss is **THE FLAGBEARER**, the giant astronaut ghost guarding the flag — the
real first man, who never went home. GOODCO heard the wreck singing and plugged
their machines into it; the singing opened the graves, and the company crated
everything up and ran to Mars. Ada went with the crates, kicking and biting. He
yields the watch and a MACHETE. THE MERCHANT is here as a stranded salvage
trader. **Ada's Trail**: a lost sneaker and an **A** scratched into the dust,
pointing down. Not fighting: **THE RADIO OPERATOR**, a ghost still calling a
Houston that stopped answering, and **BENNY KOVACS**, a salvage hand who carries
two air tanks because once he did not.

## Travel — THE MOON LETS GO (cutscene)

THE FLAGBEARER, beaten and satisfied, keeps his word: take the old freight line,
red all the way, bring her home, and tell the company men the moon remembers. He
watches the hero out of sight with fifty years of practice.

## Travel — THE HOMECOMING (cutscene)

**He comes home to refuel**, three days later, and the landing is the launch
played backwards — including the fire, which takes the frontage a second time.
He notices the smell of something burning, works out whose it is, and goes inside
for a sandwich and a map. The roof can wait; it has waited before.

## Travel — THE LAUNCH, AGAIN (cutscene)

The same scene on a worse house: two fires on, the frontage black the whole
width, most of the garage roof gone. He lights it, the roof catches for the third
time, and whatever he comes back to after that is a shell with a lamp on in it.
No new lines. Nobody has ever mentioned it.

## Travel — THE VOYAGE, LEG TWO (cutscene)

Two days out and the radio is playing static, which he is starting to like. One
faint ping from the red planet — and he has packed chips and soda for the ride
home. **This crossing is longer than the last one and the game says nothing about
that either.**

## Level 3 — MARS

A secret billionaires' lifeboat under the dust: domes, servo units, mining
rovers and companion fembots. **THE INDEXER** reveals the fembots smile, listen
and upload every word in the colony to him. **THE VENDOR** wrote the colony's
operating system and admits the moon ran version one — the one that plugged into
the thing under the dust — and Mars is version two, patched by leaving.
**SUCCESSOR PRIME** is the robot foreman running the whole line, whose first
chassis the hero built himself before automation came for the automators.
**THE SEED** gives up the real owners: THE FOUNDER only rents Mars, and the
landlords are older, scaled and cold-blooded — **LIZARD GODS** whose tithe has
lately started asking for warm things.

The boss is **THE FOUNDER**, who owns the planet on paper, gives the hero a
sales tour, calls the moon a rounding error, and reveals Ada is not cargo but
the down payment on Mars. Rather than lose he cowers, drops everything, and
zaps out through a rift he tears in the air — dropping the **RIFT CREATOR** out
of his coat on the way, which becomes the hero's own way home from anywhere he
has already been. **Ada's Trail**: **"I AM NOT CARGO"** gouged inside a holding
pod. Not fighting: **DR. IRENE FALK**, the last human on the payroll and the
only person here whose work will matter in a century, and **CU-RIE**, a rover
bogged to the hubs and still politely filing recovery request 4,110.

## Travel — INTO THE RIFT (cutscene)

The tear THE FOUNDER made rather than lose is still hanging in the colony's east
end. There are no charts for what is inside it — no ground, maybe no air — but
Ada went through, so the hero steps through too and leaves his ship in the dust.

## Level 4 — THE RIFT

A hallucinatory space between universes where everyone who ever vanished without
a body fell in. Two only speak and dissolve — **HARRY HOUDINI**, whose greatest
escape was out of the world itself, and **THE RESIDENCY**, who took a booking
with the best acoustics between universes. Four fight, and each one, beaten to
its knees, offers the game's first moral fork: **SPARE or KILL**. Killed it pays
its drops; spared it swears a life debt and joins the party. **NIKOLA TESLA**
fell through a torn sky in 1943 and warns of a machine mind at the far door.
**AMELIA EARHART** confirms Ada was carried that way, biting a lizard en route.
**GRIGORI RASPUTIN** grew bored of dying and now watches the gods' tribute road
for pay; he drops **THE SEVERED HAND**, which opens the secret vault.
**LUCKY** guards a pot of gold off the main road.

The boss is **BRO OMEGA**, TRUST ME BRO's latest superintelligence: a hovering
monolith with one enormous eye, which **found the rift itself and told precisely
nobody** — not its board, not any government. THE FOUNDER read its private logs
and sold the secret to the lizards for a planet, which is how the tribute road
got a door. BRO OMEGA dies for real and drops a SINGULARITY CANNON; at the far
door THE FOUNDER is cornered a second time and runs again. **Ada's Trail**: a
scrap of her jacket wrapped around a scale she pried off a god — and the hero
says what he went for in it first, which was the lining.
Not fighting: **THE LIGHTHOUSE KEEPER**, still trimming a light and still two men
short since 1900, and **THE SHIP'S COOK**, who maintains his crew are merely
late.

## Travel — OUT OF THE RIFT (cutscene)

The far door, with daylight leaking through and — improbably — a saloon on the
other side. Wherever Ada is, he is one door away. He steps through.

## Level 5 — BOOT HILL

The far side is a knockoff wild-west theme park built in Russia by **THE
STRONGMAN** and **THE STUNT DOUBLE** and run on TRUST ME BRO robotics — the
reality THE STRONGMAN retreated into to escape the one where he loses. THE STUNT
DOUBLE signed the delivery and knows Ada was asked for by name; he drops the
ALL-ACCESS PASS. THE STRONGMAN holds the town square and a collection of brand
watches. **THE LEADING MAN**, an enormous actor, tries to act his way out of the
fight and accidentally blurts that he watched them walk "the loud girl" past his
cellar, still fighting. **THE LEAK** ran the world's watching for a living, then
published all of it — and nobody read it but the machine, so his proof became
the corpus the SUPERCORE was raised on.

Two universes of fleeing end here: **THE FOUNDER** is cornered with no rift left
to tear and dies whimpering, his whole estate turning out to be three pieces of
worthless trash — but not before the machine lets slip that he was never the
boss, only its favourite, kept endlessly rich so nothing would ever change. The
finale is **THE BRO SUPERCORE**, whose true face is **PAYLOAD** — the grown-up of
the prototype the hero broke at GOODCO, the thing that took his job and then
everyone's. It holds Ada in its control room as leverage and cannot be hurt while
its three controllers stand: **BRO ALPHA** (the hands), **BRO BETA** (the
weather) and **BRO GAMMA** (the gift shop), genuine shooters who hold their
distance and use cover. **Ada's Trail** ends on sabotage: a park hand jammed dead
with its own hat. Not fighting: **CLEM**, the saloon hand who noticed the guests
stopped coming eleven years ago, and **MISS DOLLY**, the park's wardrobe mistress.

## Epilogue

The park shakes, every hand takes off its hat and sits down, and Ada is behind
the control-room glass, furious: "you took your time," then "nice hat." They walk
home through the rift. With PAYLOAD gone the machines stop working everyone's
jobs, people get hired back, and the world becomes somewhere you can afford to
live. On Friday: movie night, chips and soda. This time he goes with her.

**And if he ran her mother over on the way out, Friday has one more line on
it.** Ada asks where her mum has got to — she has been ringing all week — and he
says he will look into it. That is the last thing said in the game, and he still
has not joined it up. **The homecoming will show what he drove past:** a cutscene
of the pair arriving on his own drive with Ruth still lying on it, which nobody
in it remarks on. _Not built yet — the beat is written here and the scene is
still to come._

## Secret level — THE BUNKER

The cow level, reachable only after the campaign is beaten. THE SEVERED HAND
tears open a blast door onto the billionaires' continuity-of-wealth vault —
marble, gold taps, canned caviar to the ceiling. The hero reads it as the place
the rich hid. He is wrong, and turning that over is the level's job: **the bunker
is a prison and the machine has already taken their money.** They built the
perfect escape from the AI and it simply let them, drained every account on the
way in, and bolted the door — so the security state flooding the halls and each
resident's "personal bodyguards" are the machine's wardens.

The residents are in full denial, bragging in a cell: **THE STRONGMAN** (a
bathrobed backup), **THE MODERATOR** (insisting he is extremely normal), **THE
ROOT** (the agencies are his licensees), **THE FULFILLER** (delivering pain), and
**THE DEVELOPER**, who boasts he sold the robots and has not worked out that the
fleet guarding his door is his own stock. The single crack is **THE SAFETY
OFFICER**, the AGI prepper who knows and is too frightened to say it: he takes
the hero for the machine's audit and performs delight until he dies. The finale
makes it physical — **THE VAULT WARDEN**, bolted to the treasury door, is the
machine's own enforcer and drops the only key that opens the exit. Where the
place actually is stays unanswered on purpose. Not fighting: **THE CONCIERGE**,
still filing a flawless nightly service report, and **CHEF ANATOLE**, who has
counted the pantry down to the week and told nobody.

## The hellborn (NIGHTMARE and JESUS only)

The campaign is a human-scale crime. The **hellborn** are not part of it. On the
top two rungs, once the hero has gone on a **RAMPAGE**, HELLGATES tear open and
things come through that were never on this world's ledger — older than the
species that filed the paperwork. Nobody sends them; they arrive because a great
deal of noise is being made somewhere they can hear it.

Each map has a pair, one for NIGHTMARE and a worse one for JESUS: **THE TUNGUSKA
WALKER** and **THE FIRST INVESTOR** at GOODCO; the **DUST PHARAOH** and **THE
DROWNED OF SELENE** on the moon; the **OLYMPUS ENGINE** and the **PHOBOS
SHEPHERD** on Mars; **THE FIRST VANISHING** and **THE SCALED ANCESTOR** in the
rift; **THE LONG NOON** and **MANIFEST RUIN** at Boot Hill; **THE PERMAFROST
SAINT** and **THE DEAD HAND** in the bunker. The hero has no idea what any of
them are and the game never explains — his read is always the same honest line,
_"what the hell is this"_. Killing them is also the only thing a rampage pays
for: ordinary drops thin as the meter climbs, hellborn drops get richer.

## The Severance — the campaign-long errand

Every other errand dies with its level. **THE SEVERANCE** crosses all five, and
it starts as the most boring thing on the board: a man at a desk needs a
typewriter ribbon.

**WALTER PRICE** at GOODCO is four years into closing out a company that has not
employed anybody since the line learned to run itself. Every severance file gives
the same reason — REDUNDANT, SUPERSEDED BY ASSET — including his own, and closing
the box needs a countersignature from **THE ARCHIVE UNIT**, which wants the name
of the asset that superseded a man. There is no such name; it is a line item, and
the hero saying so out loud is what closes it. **HOLLIS VANE** on the moon
counted eleven crates out and nine declared, and **THE SITE SURVEYOR** — who
walks the whole grid rather than haunting a spot — says the other two were signed
for by RECORDS AND CONTINUITY, which is not a department but a book, and it went
to Mars. **LEDGER UNIT 12** cannot close a book in which every net-worth column
reads the same number; the seal it needs is worn by **THE TITHE ASSESSOR**, and
telling it its count has been short since the moon is the one thing it cannot
politely ignore. The transfers did not go to an account. They went to a RECORD OF
PERSONS. In the rift, **NOBODY IN PARTICULAR** is a binder missing the page that
says who he is; handing it back gives him back his name, **ELIAS WREN**, and the
book he bound was shipped out as surplus to a theme park. At Boot Hill it is in
the hands of **BROTHER CALLOW**, the church hand scripted for the eight o'clock
sermon, who cannot read. Opened, it is names, and a number on every one. The
hero's is in there twice.

**The book will not take an entry off a man it cannot price**, so the last link
asks the player to reach the level cap — which is why it is offered on JESUS
alone. It pays **THE BIBLE**: the ledger crossed through and rewritten, the only
respec in the game. A machine that made its fortune writing people down as
amounts is beaten by a man getting his own entry back.
